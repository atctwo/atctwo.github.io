Sometime last year during my obsession with buying obsolete industrial AV equipment on eBay, I bought three Kramer 8-by-8 VGA matrix switches for £15, being sold as not working / for parts.  They indeed did not work.  I did get them working eventually but the fix was a little silly, so I thought it might be useful to document it somewhere!

<!-- excerpt-end --> 

The specific devices I got were [Kramer VP-8x8](https://www1.kramerav.com/product/vp-8x8)s - these matrix switches have 8 VGA inputs which can each be routed to one or more of their 8 VGA outputs.  The video routing configuration can be set using a number of interfaces such as RS-232, LAN, IR, and a few others, but the easiest is probably the front panel interface.  The whole thing runs off a single IEC C14 socket.

These can normally be pretty expensive, but these days they have somewhat limited use since they're VGA[^1] so a lot of businesses are getting rid of them for cheap[^2].



When I tried out the units I had bought, none of them would turn on.  The power switch has a neon indicator which wasn't even turning on, so that suggested a power supply issue (which was technically true)!  It turned out to be a little more complex than that, so in this post I want to describe what the problem was, why I think the PSUs died, and the steps I took to get to those conclusions.

[^1]: The matrix seems to switch the RGB video lines as regular old analog signals, so in theory [you can pass whatever you like through them](https://bsky.app/profile/atctwo.net/post/3mgt5dnlvvk2g) (within the limits of the switch's bandwidth)

[^2]: This is true also for more modern equipment (eg: HDMI switches) that are just being replaced because there's a new version out or a service contract with the distributor has ended.  If you're lucky you can get some very good equipment for pretty cheap on places like eBay.

{% include admonition.html type="tip" title="TL;DR (because i wrote this bit earlier and don't want to delete it)" %}

The device is a Kramer VP-8x8 VGA Matrix Switcher.  The built-in power supply provides 5V to the mainboard, which then regulates that to both 3.3V and -5V.  The problem was with the -5V regulator (LM2596) – since it’s operating in inverting buck-boost mode, the initial inrush current is huge.  The PSU the device came with was dead when I checked it, and I suspect it was continued exposure to that inrush current that killed it.

The LM2596’s datasheet recommends using an RC delay circuit on the ON/OFF pin to delay startup until the input capacitors are ready, but the design for this device doesn’t have that; it simply pulls that pin down to -5V through a 0 ohm resistor (R190).  My modification adds a little board with the suggested RC circuit, and removes R190.  This brings the -5V reg inline with the datasheet, and it seems to work fine.  I also replaced the stock power supply with a Mean Well RS-15-5 (5V at 15W).

# Diagnosing the problem

Given that the device apparently wasn't turning on at all, I started by looking at the power supply.  My units came each with a MMC SMMCU0052 PSU (it's hard to find info on this but I did find [one eBay listing](https://www.ebay.co.uk/itm/126638771468)).  It should output `+5V,3A`, `-5V,0.25A`, `+15V,0.5A` and `-15V,0.5A`, but it looks like the VP-8x8 only uses the +5V line.

Applying power to the PSU (be careful with mains!!), I wasn't able to measure anything on any output.  The PSU was toast, but I kinda expected that.  This isn't really a big issue, since they're pretty easy to replace; I replaced mine with a [Mean Well RS-15-5](https://www.digikey.co.uk/en/products/detail/mean-well-usa-inc/RS-15-5/7706168), which only has a 5V, 3A output but that's all the matrix needs.

To make sure it was *only* the PSU that had failed, I hooked up the 5V input to a bench power supply.  When applying the power, the 5V output would instantly get dragged down to about 2.29V while the matrix seemingly did nothing.  This confirmed my fear that it wasn't just the dead PSU that was the problem.

My suspicions at this point were that something was up with the power subsystem on the mainboard.  I very roughly reverse engineered the schematic of the power regulation sections of the board to get an idea of where things *could* be going wrong.  I've tried to clean it up a bit to present here but please please please don't take it as correct, there's almost certainly transcription errors!

The 5V input is used to create two additional power lines: +3.3V and -5V.  The 3.3V rail is most likely used as power for much of the control circuitry and onboard microcontrollers, while the -5V rail is probably used for much of the analog circuitry.  These two, along with the 5V input, power everything on the board.

Of note is how the -5V regulator works.  The regulator used is an industry standard [LM2596](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/lm2596.pdf) regulator, specifically the **+**5V variant.  It's able to produce **-**5V using an *inverting configuration* suggested in the datasheet, where the voltage output is taken to be at ground level, meaning the regulator's "ground" is 5 volts lower!

(It's also worth noting that as far as I could see, the 5V input had no protection (eg: for overvoltage) other than fuse F1, at least in the power section.  I might have just missed something, but this isn't the last questionable design choice on this board...)

<figure class="cool-figure">
    <img src="/assets/images/posts/vp8x8/fig8-5.png" alt="An electronic circuit schematic (taken from a datasheet) showing a linear voltage regulator along with many supporting components">
    <figcaption>The LM2596 datasheet's recommended configuration for inverting regulation</figcaption>
</figure>

My next troubleshooting step was to remove stuff until it works, then add those things back one by one until it breaks.  I started by using a hotplate to remove the 3.3V and -5V regulators and tried the external power again.  It obviously didn't power on, but it *did* stop the bench PSU from undervolting.  I added the 3.3V reg back, and not only did the bench supply handle it, the front panel turned on!

Of course, video switching wasn't working.  But it did mean that I had isolated the problem to the -5V regulator.  I tested the LM2596 chip on it's own without any other supporting circuitry to see if it was dead, but surprisingly it worked fine!  This further suggested that the problem wasn't with the regulator, but either with *how it was being used*, or with *something that used the -5V rail*.

Hoping it was the former, I compared the inverting regulator schematic in the datasheet to what was on the mainboard.  If you were looking closely, you might have noticed some missing components from the -5V circuit.  The regulator's ON/OFF (enable) pin is tied to ground through a resistor, but rather than using the suggested 47kΩ resistor the matrix uses a 0Ω link - it's just pulled straight to ground.  You might also notice the possibly bigger issue that C1 and R1 are missing completely.

<figure class="cool-figure">
    <img src="/assets/images/posts/vp8x8/fig8-1.png" alt="An electronic circuit schematic (taken from a datasheet) showing a linear voltage regulator along with many supporting components, specifically showing the RC network on the ON/OFF pin allowing for delayed startup">
    <figcaption>The LM2596 datasheet's recommended configuration for delayed startup</figcaption>
</figure>

The LM2596 datasheet suggests putting the ON/OFF pin behind an RC delay circuit.  This gives the input voltage enough time to rise to a point where regulation can happen, but critically it also allows capacitor C42 to charge before the regulator is enabled.  The datasheet says:

> **8.3.1 Delayed Startup**
>
> ...
> 
> This delayed start-up feature is useful in situations where the input power source is limited in the amount of current it can deliver. It allows the input voltage to rise to a higher voltage before the regulator starts operating. Buck regulators require less input current at higher input voltages

My *theory* is that without the RC delay circuit, the regulator (tries to) turn on immediately.  With C42 not being fully charged, the full inrush current has to be sourced by the root power supply - that's what was messing up my bench supply, and I'm thinking that *might* be what killed the stock PSUs installed in the devices.

Sure enough, adding the suggested power-on delay circuit allowed the -5V reg to start up with no issues, meaning that for the first time the whole matrix was working!

# The repair

To create a more permanent fix for the devices, I created a few little "patch boards" using some veroboard.  They each have three wires that connect to +5V, the regulator's ground (ie: -5V), and the ON/OFF pin.  Also, resistor R190 is removed completely to prevent the new R2 being bypassed.

<figure class="cool-figure">
    <img src="/assets/images/posts/vp8x8/patch.jpg" alt="Two delay circuit patch boards, one showing the top and one showing the bottom.  They are small brown circuit boards with one capacitor, two resistors, and three long wires coming out of one side">
    <figcaption>The RC delay patch boards</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="cool-figure">
    <img src="/assets/images/posts/vp8x8/-5v-fixed.png" alt="The same -5V regulator schematic as shown before, except with a blue box added in the lower left, with three components and three connections going off to the main circuit">
    <figcaption>Schematic of the board showing how it's wired in</figcaption>
</figure>

It doesn't really matter whereabout the wires are connected, but I found it convenient to connect them to these places:
- the +5V line goes to one of the pins on the main AVCC input connector footprint
- the -5V line gets soldered on to the -5V regulator's "ground" plane, which really becomes a -5V power plane
- the ON/OFF wire gets soldered directly to the ON/OFF pin (pin 5)

I mounted the board and its wires with hot glue.  Since the bottom of the board has exposed solder joints, I covered the mounting area of the main board in kapton tape first.

After installing the patch board (**and also installing a new power supply**), the matrix worked perfectly!

# It works!
That's about it!  In the end it turned to be a pretty simple fix for a kinda obscure design oversight, and I'm amazed I got it working at all.

I repaired the first one and it worked perfectly, then forgot to repair the other two for like a year.  The thing that got me to actually fix these was that I wanted to donate two of them to the [Swap Shop](https://www.emfcamp.org/about/swap-shop) at EMF2026, and I didn't want to donate broken equipment when I knew how to fix them.  (If you got to this post from a label on one of these: hi!!)

Anyway, I hope this helped restore some kinda useless AV equipment, or at least that you enjoyed the read!
